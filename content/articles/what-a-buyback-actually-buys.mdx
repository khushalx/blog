A buyback announcement has an unusually reassuring sound. A company has cash. It wants to buy its own shares. Surely that is good news for the people who own them.

Sometimes it is. But imagine a company you admire buying another business without telling you the price. You would probably wait for that detail before deciding whether the deal made sense.

Buying its own shares deserves the same scrutiny.

On [28 September 2026, NVIDIA announced](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-a-150-Billion-Share-Repurchase-Authorization-Increase/default.aspx) an additional **$150 billion of share repurchase authorisation**, taking its remaining authorised amount to **$235 billion**. The company said it expected to execute that remaining programme through fiscal 2028.

The scale makes the announcement interesting. The more useful question is what those dollars buy for a shareholder who stays.

## First, separate permission from purchases

An authorisation sets a budget within which a company can repurchase shares. It is not a receipt for money already spent. NVIDIA’s execution timetable is an expectation, rather than a completed transaction.

For evidence of actual purchases, turn to the accounts. Its [latest quarterly filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1045810/000104581026000075/nvda-20260726.htm), covering the quarter ended 26 July 2026, reports **$19.7 billion spent repurchasing about 94 million shares**.

Those July transactions and the September authorisation answer different questions. One records what happened during a finished quarter; the other describes room for future purchases. Dividing the new budget by today’s share price would produce a hypothetical share count, not an execution forecast.

That distinction matters before any discussion of whether the programme is attractive.

## Who actually receives the money?

In a buyback, cash goes to shareholders who sell. If you keep your shares, you do not receive that cash payment. Instead, when repurchased shares are retired, your unchanged holding represents a larger percentage of the shares left.

Consider a fictional business with 100 shares. You own one. It buys back and retires ten, leaving 90. Your ownership rises from 1% to roughly 1.11%, even though you did nothing.

That is the appealing part. There is a second part: the business spent cash to make it happen. You now own more of a company with less cash.

Whether that exchange improves your position depends on the value obtained for the money spent. The smaller denominator is only half the story.

## The earnings number can improve in all three cases

Earnings per share, or EPS, divides profit by a share count. Hold profit constant and reduce the denominator, and EPS rises. No extra products need to be sold.

Try the example below. The buyback budget and annual profit stay fixed. Change the price paid, then compare the earnings figure with the estimated value of each remaining share.

At ₹100, the company spends ₹1,000 crore to retire ten crore shares. EPS rises from ₹10 to ₹11.11. Yet its assumed equity value also falls from ₹10,000 crore to ₹9,000 crore as cash leaves. Dividing that by the 90 crore shares remaining still gives **₹100 per share**.

Now select ₹200. EPS rises again, to ₹10.53, but assumed value per remaining share falls to **₹94.74**. The company used the same cash to buy fewer shares at twice the starting estimate of their worth.

At ₹80, the direction reverses. More shares are retired for the same budget, leaving more estimated value attached to each continuing share.

The difficult part in real investing is estimating that starting value. It is not printed in the annual report. The model explains the relationship; it does not tell us whether NVIDIA, or any other company, is currently undervalued.

## Then check whether the share count really shrank

Gross repurchases are not the same as the net reduction in shares. Companies can issue shares too, including through employee compensation. Some repurchase spending may offset that issuance rather than reduce the overall count.

This does not make stock compensation automatically unreasonable. It does mean the headline spend cannot establish how much your ownership increased. Look at shares issued, shares repurchased and the resulting shares outstanding together.

There is also a timing issue. A December purchase does not affect a full year’s reported EPS in the same way as a January purchase. Reported EPS uses **weighted average shares**, and diluted EPS also considers potentially dilutive securities. NVIDIA’s filing explicitly sets out that calculation in Note 3.

Our example assumes purchases happen before the year starts and there is no new issuance. That makes the mechanism easy to see. Actual filings require the extra steps.

## Cash has other possible jobs

A repurchase competes with reinvestment, acquisitions, dividends, debt repayment and keeping a reserve. Spending surplus cash on sensibly priced shares can be attractive. Spending cash needed to maintain the business is a different decision.

I would want to know whether management has funded the investments that sustain future earnings, and whether the balance sheet remains resilient afterwards. A company can look comfortable in a strong year and find that flexibility more valuable when demand weakens.

The assumption of unchanged profit also deserves attention. Cash may have earned interest. Borrowing to fund a buyback introduces interest costs and financial risk. Those effects can reduce or reverse the tidy EPS increase in a simplified example.

This is where a buyback becomes a capital-allocation decision, rather than a decorative addition to an earnings release.

## What I would read after the headline

For the next quarterly report, four checks are more useful than the size of the announcement alone:

1. **Execution:** how much was actually spent, over which period, and at what average price?
2. **Ownership:** after issuance and repurchases, what happened to the share count?
3. **Affordability:** what cash remains after the business’s investment needs and obligations?
4. **Price discipline:** what supports the view that repurchasing shares was an attractive use of that cash?

NVIDIA’s announcement gives us a large, timely example. It does not answer all four questions in advance.

The habit worth keeping is simple: when a company buys its own shares, assess it as you would any substantial purchase. Ask what it paid, what it received, and what it gave up.

A larger ownership percentage can be valuable. Paying too much to obtain it can still be a poor trade.

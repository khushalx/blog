Imagine a footwear business that sells a pair of shoes for ₹5,000. The product costs it records as cost of sales come to ₹3,000. It has ₹2,000 left: a gross margin of 40%.

That money still has work to do. It must help pay for marketing, administration and other expenses before we get to the profit available to shareholders. It is not ₹2,000 of spare cash in the bank.

Now suppose the business becomes a little more efficient. It keeps a larger share of each sale, but sells less overall. Has profitability improved?

There are two answers hiding inside that question: how much the company keeps **from each rupee of revenue**, and how much it keeps **in total**. Nike’s latest results are a useful example of the difference.

## A stronger percentage, a smaller pool

In its [results released on 1 October 2026](https://investors.nike.com/investors/news-events-and-reports/investor-news/investor-news-details/2026/NIKE-Inc--Reports-Fiscal-2027-First-Quarter-Results/default.aspx), Nike reported a higher gross margin for the quarter ended 31 August. It also reported lower revenue and lower gross profit.

The margin improved by **0.6 percentage points**, or 60 basis points. Meanwhile, the dollar amount of gross profit fell by **$145 million**, about 2.9%, calculated from the reported amounts.

There is no accounting contradiction. Gross profit is revenue minus cost of sales. Gross margin divides that profit by revenue. A smaller pool can represent a bigger share of a smaller revenue base.

The ratio improved. The amount available to cover everything below cost of sales did not.

## Why the sales decline matters

It helps to write the relationship in the other direction:

**Gross profit = revenue × gross margin.**

If revenue falls, margin has to improve enough to compensate. An improvement that looks encouraging in isolation may be too small to do that.

Using Nike’s reported figures, maintaining the prior-year quarter’s gross profit of $4,943 million on revenue of $11,213 million would have required a gross margin of about **44.08%**. That is an arithmetic threshold, calculated as 4,943 divided by 11,213. It is not management guidance or a forecast.

The reported 42.8% was better than before, but below that threshold. Published margins are rounded, so use the reported dollar figures for precise calculations rather than multiplying rounded percentages and expecting an exact match.

In the starting example, sales fall from ₹100 crore to ₹90 crore while margin rises from 40% to 42%. Gross profit still declines from ₹40 crore to ₹37.80 crore.

With sales at ₹90 crore, raise the margin to 44.5%. The profit pool edges above its old level. Or restore sales to ₹100 crore while keeping the improved margin. These are different paths to the same question: how much money is left after cost of sales?

Real businesses cannot move these controls independently. Raising prices may change demand; reducing costs may affect quality or delivery. The example isolates the arithmetic so those commercial trade-offs are easier to recognise.

## What improved the margin?

A higher margin could reflect stronger pricing, cheaper inputs, fewer discounts, a different product mix or a change in distribution. Those explanations carry different implications for future earnings.

Nike’s [quarterly filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/320187/000032018726000193/nke-20260831.htm) provides a bridge between the two margins. Lower warehousing and logistics costs added approximately 90 basis points, while favourable foreign-exchange effects, including hedges, added around 40. Other factors offset part of those gains. Higher discounts and channel mix contributed to lower average selling prices, reducing margin by around 30 basis points.

That matters because the overall increase cannot simply be read as evidence that customers were willing to pay more. Cost improvements and currency helped even while some selling-price effects worked against the company.

The cost improvement may be useful and durable. Currency effects may change. Pricing and product mix require a separate look. One percentage combines several stories, and the notes help pull them apart.

  When a company reports an improved margin, put three things beside it: revenue, the absolute profit amount, and the explanation for the change. Together, they tell you more than the percentage alone.
</ResearchCallout>

## Gross profit still has bills to pay

Gross profit is an intermediate measure. It does not tell us the final earnings outcome, and it is not operating cash flow.

A smaller pool becomes harder to manage if advertising, salaries and administrative costs do not fall with sales. Some spending supports the business over several years; some is difficult to reduce quickly. A company that loses 10% of revenue cannot necessarily remove 10% of every expense.

Nike’s selling and administrative expense did fall, from **$4,016 million to $3,910 million**, according to the earnings release. That reduction partly offset the lower gross profit. It is a reminder to keep reading down the income statement rather than treating one subtotal as the whole result.

The reverse situation is possible too. A company can grow gross profit while spending heavily enough elsewhere that net income falls. Better product economics and better final earnings are related, but they are separate observations.

## When a smaller business might be a healthier one

There is another reason to resist a quick verdict. A business might deliberately stop selling low-quality, heavily discounted products, or leave customers whose orders are costly to fulfil. Revenue can decline while the economics of the remaining business improve.

That can be a sensible decision. The question is whether the eventual benefit justifies the lost sales and whether the remaining operation can support its costs. A rising margin offers a clue, not the answer.

For a consumer brand, I would look for evidence that customers are buying the products management wants to sell, at prices that support attractive economics. I would also check whether inventory and discounting are improving without shifting the problem into another channel or period.

Those are questions for further research, not conclusions established by Nike’s gross-margin increase.

## What I would watch next

Three checks would make the next result more informative:

1. **Does the profit pool recover?** Compare absolute gross profit with both revenue and margin. A better ratio is more persuasive when it supports more dollars too.
2. **What is driving the change?** Distinguish product and pricing improvements from logistics savings, currency and mix effects.
3. **Can the rest of the business support it?** Follow operating expenses, inventory and cash generation rather than stopping at the gross-profit line.

Nike’s results do not make higher margins unimportant. They show why margins need context.

The percentage tells you what the business retains from each unit of revenue. The profit pool tells you how much it has to work with. Reading both is a small habit that makes an earnings report much more useful.

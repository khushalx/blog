## Start with the operating cycle

To understand return on capital employed, think about a physical business: it needs facilities, equipment and stock before it can earn revenue. ROCE connects operating profit to the capital committed to that activity.

This sample uses a **hypothetical manufacturer**, not reported results from a real company. Suppose it earns ₹20 crore of annual operating profit and uses average capital employed of ₹100 crore. Under a simple EBIT divided by average capital employed definition, ROCE is 20%.

## The next rupee matters

Now suppose an expansion requires another ₹50 crore and eventually adds ₹5 crore of operating profit. The incremental return is 10%, even though the original business earned 20%. Blending the two can conceal the declining attractiveness of new investment.

<ResearchCallout>
  Historical ROCE describes what the existing capital base has earned. A growth
  decision depends on what the next unit of capital can earn.
</ResearchCallout>

## Definitions before comparisons

Different analysts adjust cash, leases, goodwill and exceptional profits differently. A credible comparison states the definition, uses consistent periods and considers where the company sits in its investment cycle.

Capital spent on an unfinished facility can temporarily depress returns. Conversely, an old asset base with low book values can make reported returns look unusually strong. Neither observation substitutes for examining cash economics.

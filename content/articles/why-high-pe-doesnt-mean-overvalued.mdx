A company trading at 40 times earnings looks expensive next to one trading at 12 times. The comparison is immediate, intuitive and incomplete.

The price-to-earnings ratio tells us how much the market is paying for a unit of earnings. It does not tell us how dependable those earnings are, how much cash must be reinvested to sustain them, or how long they can grow. Those questions determine whether the price is reasonable.

**A high P/E is a prompt to investigate expectations. It is not, by itself, a verdict.**

## First, understand the denominator

P/E divides the price per share by earnings per share. Before comparing two ratios, establish whether both use trailing earnings, forward estimates or some adjusted measure. Otherwise, the apparently precise comparison may be between different things.

Earnings can also be temporarily depressed. A cyclical company near the bottom of its cycle can show a high P/E simply because profits are unusually low. At the top of the cycle, the same company may appear cheap because profits are unusually strong. Neither observation tells us what a normal year looks like.

One-off gains create a different distortion. Selling an asset can increase reported profit without improving the recurring earning power of the business. A low multiple of that profit can be misleading.

> The number is a summary. The assumptions underneath it are the analysis.

This is the central idea of this essay, not a quotation attributed to an investor.

## Two businesses, one starting profit

Consider two **invented businesses**, each earning ₹10 per share today. Company A trades at ₹200, or 20 times earnings. Company B trades at ₹350, or 35 times earnings. Neither represents an actual listed company.

For illustration, suppose A’s earnings grow at 5% annually for five years, while B’s grow at 15%. Assume a fixed share count and no dividends. These are scenario assumptions, not forecasts.

B’s faster earnings growth narrows the initial valuation difference. But this does not establish that B is a better investment. Its initial price already anticipates a stronger business outcome.

Suppose both companies trade at 20 times earnings in Year 5. A’s illustrative price would be about ₹255, a roughly 5% annualised price return. B’s would be about ₹402, a roughly 2.8% annualised price return. Despite faster earnings growth, B delivers a lower price return in this particular scenario because its multiple contracts.

Now change only B’s ending multiple to 30 times. Its illustrative price becomes about ₹603, and its annualised price return rises to roughly 11.5%. The exercise demonstrates how sensitive the result is to the assumptions. It does not tell us which ending multiple is appropriate.

## Growth has a funding requirement

Two businesses can report similar earnings growth while creating very different amounts of value.

Imagine one needs to reinvest nearly all its operating cash to achieve that growth. The other can grow with modest incremental investment and distribute the rest. The second may support a higher valuation, but only if those economics are durable and the risks are comparable.

This is why the return on incremental capital matters. Revenue growth alone cannot tell us whether expansion is worthwhile. Growth funded by investments earning less than their cost can reduce value even as the company becomes larger.

For a capital-intensive company, the gap between accounting earnings and cash available to owners deserves particular attention. Depreciation is an accounting allocation; replacement expenditure is a cash requirement. They need not be equal.

## Durability changes the calculation

A year of high growth and a decade of high growth are different propositions. Valuation depends partly on how long a business can sustain favourable economics before competition, saturation or technological change reduces them.

Durability is easy to describe and hard to establish. A strong historical record is useful evidence, but it is not a guarantee. Ask what mechanism supports the returns: switching costs, efficient distribution, lower operating costs, customer trust, or something else. Then ask how that mechanism could weaken.

The more years of success required to justify the price, the more consequential the uncertainty becomes. This is one reason an apparently excellent business can still offer little room for error.

## A more useful set of questions

Rather than asking only whether a P/E looks high, work through a short sequence:

1. **Are the earnings representative?** Look for cyclical peaks, exceptional items and accounting changes.
2. **What cash reaches owners?** Consider reinvestment, working capital, dilution and debt obligations.
3. **What growth is implied?** Make the expectation explicit instead of relying on a general story.
4. **How long must it last?** Separate a near-term recovery from a durable competitive advantage.
5. **What would invalidate the case?** Identify the observations that would require a change of mind.

## A multiple is a compressed argument

A P/E ratio is useful because it is simple. Its limitation is exactly the same. It compresses assumptions about future earnings, cash generation, risk and time into one number.

The goal is not to defend every expensive stock, or dismiss cheap ones. It is to avoid confusing a low number with a margin of safety and a high number with certainty of overvaluation.

Start with the multiple. Then unpack the argument it contains.

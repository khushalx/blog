You can be right about a business and still be disappointed by its shares. That sounds unfair until you remember that you do not buy a company’s quality in isolation. You buy it at a price.

An annual report can show that sales are growing, profits are strong and cash is coming in. It cannot tell you whether those good results were already expected when you bought the stock. That second question is about the *share-price outcome*, and it requires a different kind of thinking.

## What the business can tell us

Microsoft is a useful current example. In its [fiscal 2026 Form 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/789019/000119312526323660/msft-20260630.htm), the company reported $331.8 billion of revenue, $155.2 billion of operating income and $182.9 billion of cash from operations. Each was higher than in fiscal 2025. Operating income was about 47% of revenue.

Those are substantial operating results. They show scale and profitability, though they are not a complete verdict on the durability of the business. Microsoft also spent much more on property and equipment in 2026, a reminder that future cash generation depends partly on what its current investment earns.

None of these figures tells us what return a buyer of Microsoft shares will earn from **today’s** price. This article makes no claim that the shares are cheap or expensive. The filing helps us examine the business; a share-price decision also needs a view of future results *and* the price being paid for them.

## Two investors, one identical company

To isolate the price question, leave Microsoft behind for a moment. Imagine a **fictional company** earning ₹10 per share today. Its earnings per share grow by 12% a year for five years, reaching about ₹17.62. Both investors know this in advance. At the end of year five, the market values the company at 25 times earnings, giving it a share price of about ₹440.59.

The only difference is the entry price. One investor buys at 20 times today’s earnings, or ₹200. The other pays 40 times, or ₹400. Try switching between them:

The business does exactly the same thing for both people. Their ending share price is the same. The buyer at ₹200 sees roughly **17.1% annualised price growth**; the buyer at ₹400 sees about **2.0%**. Both owned a growing business. Only one bought it at a price that left much room for a satisfying outcome under these assumptions.

This is **illustrative arithmetic**, not a forecast, a fair-value estimate or a disguised view on Microsoft. The exit multiple of 25 is an assumption, not a fact we can know today. Dividends, taxes, trading costs and changes in share count are excluded.

## Why the multiple can move

A share price is often described as earnings multiplied by a valuation multiple. That is a useful shorthand: **price ≈ earnings per share × P/E**. Earnings describe one part of what the business delivers. The P/E reflects how much investors are willing to pay for those earnings at a particular moment.

That willingness can change even if the company keeps growing. Investors might revise their view of how long growth can last. Interest rates may change the appeal of future profits. Competitors may appear. Or an unusually high starting price may simply have assumed an even better future than the company delivered.

In our fictional example, earnings grow 76% over five years. Yet the investor who paid 40 times earnings gets only a modest price gain because the market’s multiple falls to 25 times. The company did not need to fail for the investment to disappoint.

The opposite is possible too. A modest business can produce a strong share-price return if bought cheaply and if the outcome is better than the price implied. That does **not** make a weak business safe: debt, poor cash conversion or declining economics can still overwhelm a low-looking multiple.

## The question after “Is it a good business?”

Business quality deserves careful study. Look for evidence that customers return, that margins are resilient, that growth earns an adequate return on the capital it needs, and that reported profit becomes cash over time. Microsoft’s latest annual filing gives us facts to investigate along those lines; its sharply higher equipment spending is one of the questions, not a conclusion by itself.

Then make the price question explicit:

1. **What performance does today’s price seem to require?** A wonderful company can be a poor purchase if an extraordinary future is already assumed.
2. **What happens if the business does well, but the valuation multiple falls?** Run the numbers with a less generous exit price.
3. **What could change the business economics?** A good historical record is evidence, not protection from competition or costly reinvestment.
4. **What reaches the shareholder?** Include dividends, dilution, buybacks and taxes when moving from a simple price example to a real investment outcome.

It is tempting to call a company “good” and let that settle the investment case. It settles only half of it. The business has to perform; the purchase price has to leave room for that performance to matter.
